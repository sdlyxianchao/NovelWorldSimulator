# Chapter 3 — Tree Shadows

Before the day had brightened, Lu Xingzhou was already at the pass.

The old tree grew beside the stone steps; its trunk would take two men to span. He set himself under it, facing east, with a small stone pressed into the ground at his feet.

"What are you standing here for?" A Ruo came after him.

"Waiting for the shadow."

The sun rose. The tree's shadow spread from the west, first a small patch, then slowly drawn longer. When the edge of the shadow met the small stone, Lu Xingzhou looked up at the sun, then down at the shadow, and wrote the hour in the ledger.

"Third quarter of the chen hour," he said.

He drew a line on the ground at the edge of the shadow with the charcoal and marked the hour. The wind passed; the shadow stirred, then moved itself back.

"When did you put the stone here?"

"Yesterday."

He moved to the next tree. A few steps, then stand, then up, then down, then write. Done, he went on. Where the next tree was, he did not ask A Ruo, and he did not look at the path. On some trees the shadow fell into the stream — he recorded the edge on the bank first, then the edge in the water.

There was one on a slope whose shadow would not stop until it reached the ridge. He waited a good while before he drew the line.

"That one you haven't recorded." A Ruo pointed it out.

"I have." He turned the ledger for her to see. In the corner of the page was that tree, marked with the noon hour.

A Ruo looked a long time and did not recognize the tree she had pointed to.

"None of your trees look alike," she said.

"Alike doesn't matter," he said. "What I want is the hour the shadow lands."

The whole morning he went from the pass to halfway up, and the ledger grew thicker. At noon he rested by the stream and laid the ledger on his knee, checking the numbers from the beginning.

"You know the way," A Ruo said.

"I do."

"You've never walked it."

"I have," Lu Xingzhou said. "The day your steward came to hire me, he took me around first. From the pass to the back of the mountain, a whole day's walk."

A Ruo remembered. That day she had seen a stranger come up the mountain. She had only not stopped him.

"Once the shadows are recorded," she asked, "can the mountain come back?"

"It's the shape that comes back," he said. "If the shadows are right, the shape matches. If it matches, then it is as it was when borrowed."

"As it was when borrowed." A Ruo repeated it. She did not understand, and she did not ask again.

On the third morning he went to the rock wall first. He wedged the rule back into yesterday's seam and checked it against the charcoal line.

"Hasn't moved." He put the rule away.

At the stream he counted the folds and checked the ledger again. "Hasn't moved either."

"Is not moving a good thing?" A Ruo asked.

"It's good," he said. "A thing that's borrowed gets matched piece by piece first, so that when it goes back nothing is off."

Late in the afternoon they went up to the back of the mountain. The trees there were shorter than the ones in front, their branches growing sideways, and so did their shadows, packed together, layer over layer. Lu Xingzhou picked the densest one and walked three circles around it before he settled.

"This one is hard to record," he said.

"It's the hardest," A Ruo said. "Its shadow lies over the others'."

"Still has to be recorded."

He crouched and drew the root of the shadow first, then the point of it.

The sun sank west and the shadows stretched longer and longer. When he had recorded the last tree he stood at the highest point of the back of the mountain and turned the ledger from the first page to the last, then back again, a page at a time.

"It's done," he said.

"Tomorrow?" A Ruo asked.

"Tomorrow I don't record."

"Not record?"

"It's done." He closed the ledger. "The rest is seeing whether it matches."

A Ruo looked at the trees. The wind passed, and the shadows swayed together.

"They remember you," she said suddenly.

"Why would they remember me."

"You came up once," A Ruo said. "Whatever they've seen, they remember."

Lu Xingzhou put the ledger inside his coat, next to the folded contract.

"Tomorrow," he said, "stay in the house. Don't come out."

"Why?"

"I can't say for certain." He turned and went down the mountain. "Three days of records — on the fourth day they start to be used."

A Ruo stood at the back pass and watched him go down. A thin warmth hung over the path. Light came through the gaps in the trees, falling on his back, and on her too. Under her feet was nothing at all.
