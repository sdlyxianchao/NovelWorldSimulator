# Chapter 2 — The Shadow Ledger

A Ruo caught up and planted herself in front of him, blocking the path.

"What are you going up for?"

"To borrow something."

"Borrow what?"

"The mountain's shadow."

A Ruo frowned. "A mountain has no shadow."

"It does," Lu Xingzhou said. "Tilt the sun and it tilts. You want it to stand in the way, but the mountain cannot move itself. The shadow is all there is to borrow."

She said nothing, and she did not step aside. He went around her, put his feet on the stone steps, and climbed one at a time.

Halfway up there was a flat stone. He sat down and laid the things out of his cloth bag one by one: a hand of bamboo rule, a length of hemp cord, a stick of charcoal, and a paper-bound ledger whose corners were worn soft, as though it had been used for many years.

Above was a wall of rock, crossed by several seams. He wedged the rule into the widest seam and narrowed his eyes.

"A finger and a half." He drew a mark on the wall with the charcoal. "Noted."

"What is that for?" A Ruo asked behind him.

"Seams move. A finger of seam, a fraction of tilt. When it comes back it has to match, so first I have to know how wide it is now."

"The seams of a whole mountain — can you finish them?"

"I can finish them," he said. "There are only so many."

He did not measure fast. When he had finished a seam he drew a line across the wall with the charcoal, marked the number, then turned to the ledger and sketched a small figure of it. A crude figure — two bends, a narrow seam between.

"Why draw it? Isn't the number enough?" A Ruo asked.

"Numbers get confused," he said. "Pictures don't."

At the east end of the wall there was a seam that split from top to bottom, longer than the rule. He laid the hemp cord along it, end to end, then took the cord down and measured it section by section.

"Four feet two," he noted.

A Ruo watched a while, then went and measured a seam beside it. "One finger."

"Right," he said. "That one was one finger yesterday too."

The whole morning he spent under the rock wall. Measure one, draw one, note one. A Ruo followed him, watching him move from the east end to the west, the rule going in and out, the charcoal leaving a row of marks on the wall.

At noon he took half a dry ration from the bag and broke off a piece for her. A Ruo did not take it. He ate it himself and went down to the stream.

The water ran against the stones, bent once, bent again. Lu Xingzhou crouched at the bank and followed a leaf downstream, counting under his breath: "One. Two. Three…"

The leaf turned twice, caught on the stone, and slid into the next fold.

At nineteen he stopped and wrote the number in the ledger.

A Ruo crouched too and counted for herself. "Seven bends."

"Seven on the surface," he said. "Nineteen underneath. Water goes where the stone goes — as many ridges as the stone has, so many times the water folds. You keep the mountain; you look at its face. I borrow it; I look at its bottom."

A Ruo drew her hand across the water. The folds on the surface broke apart; the ones underneath stayed.

He rinsed the bamboo rule in the stream, wiped it dry on his sleeve, and put it back in the bag. As he reached in, he turned his palm up for a look. From the base of the fingers to the wrist ran old marks, one after another, a shade lighter than the skin around them. He began counting at the left hand, reached his right wrist, stopped, closed his hand, and tucked it back into his sleeve.

"What are you counting?" A Ruo asked.

"Nothing." He stood up.

By dark he was back at the flat stone halfway up. He opened the ledger to a new page, wrote down the first thing to record tomorrow, and closed it.

"The shadows," A Ruo said. "Which tree first?"

"The one at the pass."

A Ruo thought it over. "It's too old."

"Old is good," Lu Xingzhou said. "The older it is, the truer the shadow."

He looked down the mountain. Over on the Cloudbrook side the lamps were coming on, one by one, in a row, all the way to the pass.

"After tonight," he said, "six days left."

A Ruo looked that way too. "Still early," she said.

Lu Xingzhou turned to her and did not answer.

The lamps reached the pass. One had not kept up. It stayed dark.

"That lamp is out," he said.

"It was lit," A Ruo said.

"When?"

"Before you came."

Lu Xingzhou looked at the dark place a long while. Wind came up through the pass and stirred the corner of the paper in his coat. He pressed the paper back down.
