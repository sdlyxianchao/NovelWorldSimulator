# Chapter 3 — The Old Piles

In the third year it rained on the night of the festival.

Few people set lanterns out in the rain, and the landing was less crowded than in other
years. By the time A Su got there the boat was still in, the line not yet loosed. Chen Du
sat in the bow and waited.

"Few people this year," she said.

"Mm."

She got in and sat in her old place. He propped one corner of the awning up to keep the rain
out. Three of the lanterns along the awning were lit, to show the water ahead.

The rain beat on the awning, thick and close. Neither of them spoke. At the bend he slowed
and pointed the oar at the water.

"There are old piles under this stretch."

"How old?"

"Taken off the bridge piers. The generation before us drove them. They show only when the
water is low."

She looked down. The water was black. She could see nothing.

"How do you know?"

"I hit them," he said. "The bottom has been patched three times."

He carried the oar to one side and took the boat along close to the water. Only when the
stretch was behind them did he straighten up.

"Where there is a pile under the water, the surface bulges," he said. "Next time, watch for
the bulge."

"I cannot see under it."

"Watch the surface."

She nodded.

The ordinary days of that third year went on at the landing as before.

In her shop A Su embroidered lamp flowers for every season: in spring, soft ones and short
ones; in summer, big ones; in autumn, close ones; in winter, small ones. She stitched only
half the day, and spent the other half winding and sorting thread. The thread had to run
smooth; a rough one tangled. The shop faced a water lane; at its mouth someone would call for
the ferry. The call came through the curtain, and her hands did not stop.

She kept no apprentice. People told her to take one; she said that with more eyes on the
stitches, the hand goes wrong.

In summer the water rose and the landing stood still for two days. Chen Du hauled the boat
out, turned it over, and scraped the silt from the bottom. The gash the old piles had opened,
he patched over again. When that was done, he counted the lanterns along the awning.

In autumn the water fell and the old stock showed on the riverbed. He tried the oar stretch by
stretch and noted where it ran deep. Where the water was deep there were no piles, and the
boat went steady.

A Su did not ask how many days were left to the festival. People at the landing asked Chen Du;
he said he did not know. Only in the month before the festival did she draw the curtain
tighter and lay the threads out, and look at them one by one.

In the fourth year there was no rain on the festival.

There were more people at the landing than in other years. The noise was all upriver; this
stretch was still black and still. The last crossing was still the two of them.

When she got in, she had one more thing with her — a cloth bundle, smaller than in other
years.

"What is this?"

"Lamp flowers."

"For whom?"

"For no one."

She set the bundle down on the boards and did not open it. He did not ask.

At the bend he slowed again.

"Last time I showed you the surface — do you remember?"

"I remember."

"Which stretch?"

She pointed. He nodded.

The boat passed the stretch; he shipped the oar and let it drift a while.

"That star at the top," she said suddenly. "It is there every year."

"The one over the bend."

"How do you know it is the same one?"

"It does not move," he said. "The others move. It does not."

She looked up, and looked a long time. The boat drifted. The star did not move.

A little before dawn she opened the bundle a crack and let him see. Inside was one lamp flower,
red with grey in it.

"Why do you not put them in the water?"

"In the water they dissolve."

"On cloth they do not?"

"On cloth they do not."

Before the boat came ashore, he said one thing.

"Whatever is said on the boat, nobody mentions the next day."

"I know," she said.

"Knowing it, you still do not mention it."

She stepped off and held the bundle close. He loosed the line, turned the boat, and went back.

On the bank a man asked him who he had carried on the last crossing of the year. He said, no
strangers.

The man asked again, then who had he carried. He said, someone who embroiders lanterns.

The man meant to ask more; he had already rowed away.

A Su went back to the shop and dropped the curtain. She tipped out that year's lamp flowers and
counted them, one by one. More were left unsold than in other years. At the last one she
stopped.

Its petal looked as though something were missing.

She turned it over, pressed it to the bottom of the box, and did not set it out on the table.

Outside the window it was daylight. Over at the landing, the lanterns hung along the boat's
awning went out, one by one.
