# Chapter 2 — Three Hundred and Sixty-Four Days

The day after the Lantern Festival, A Su opened the shop as usual.

The curtain came down and shut half the street out. On the table lay the dozen lamp flowers
left unsold from the night before, set in two rows. She counted them once and put them away
in a box.

The river was quiet. The lanterns of the night before had soaked a whole night through and
sunk before dawn; only the bamboo frames still floated. Downstream a man punted a small boat
to fish the frames out, a basketful at a time, and spread them on the bank to dry for
firewood.

The landing was quiet too. Chen Du put the boat back in its old place, unshipped the oar,
wiped the gunwale. He had rowed several dozen crossings the night before; the skin of his
palm was worn pale. He looped the line twice around the post, undid it, and looped it again.

A man came with empty baskets and called to him.

"Chen Du. One crossing."

"One crossing."

A boatful of people, two baskets of greens, a pig. Out in midstream the pig squealed, and
everyone on the boat laughed. Chen Du did not laugh. He eased the oar over a little and went
around a stretch of water.

There are old piles under that stretch. He remembers.

That year, A Su did not count the days. She counted lamp flowers.

The days in the shop were all the same length. Open up, drop the curtain, thread the needle;
put the needle down at dark, take it up at dawn. One lamp flower was three hundred and sixty
stitches, and one row counted wrong meant unpicking the whole thing. She stitched slowly, and
unpicked more slowly still.

One lamp flower took half a day. The rounds on the table grew into a small stack, sold off,
and grew again. The buyers were of every kind: for a wedding they wanted red, and she gave
them red, red with a little grey in it; for burning at a death they wanted small, and she
gave them small; some said nothing at all, looked once, and left.

Her lamp flowers do not go in the water. Someone said that only in the river would they work;
she said no. The one asked why. She said no why.

In spring a freight boat came to the landing. A Su's lamp flowers went aboard it and crossed
to the far bank to be sold. The one who carried the baskets rode Chen Du's boat. The baskets
sat at the bow under a cloth. As Chen Du rowed, the wind lifted a corner and showed a corner
of red. He glanced at it and tucked the corner back down. The accounts from the far bank came
back with the carrier: one slip of paper, with the money on it, and nothing else.

In summer the river rose. The stone steps of the landing drowned two steps deep. Chen Du tied
the boat high and kept watch all day. If someone came to cross at night, he rowed. On
heavy-rain days two people would ride together and neither spoke, listening to the rain on
the awning. A row of lanterns hung along the awning; by day they were unlit, and when the wind
blew, one knocked against the next, and rang.

In autumn the water fell. The landing was dredged, and half a boat of rotten timber came up.
Chen Du knew a few of the pieces by sight: old stock taken off the bridge piers. He set those
aside, dried them, and laid them in the bottom of the boat.

In winter the riverside families began to build lanterns — a whole street of them, all the way
down to the landing. A Su did not build lanterns; she embroidered lamp flowers. For the month
before the festival the shop was at its busiest. She drew the curtain tighter and set the
threads out in three rows — red, yellow, white, and one more she mixed herself, a colour she
could not name. When the thread ran out she went into town to buy more, and took the land
route. She did not take the boat.

People at the landing asked Chen Du how many days were left to the festival. He said he did
not know. But he remembered which years it had rained on the festival, and which years it had
not.

That year's last crossing was still the two of them.

She got in, sat down, and held the cloth bundle close. He loosed the line and took up the oar.
At the bend he slowed, and the oar stopped in the water for a beat.

"Last year's lanterns — a few soaked through," she said.

"Mm."

"This year, you carried one more."

He did not answer. Along the awning hung a row of lanterns, each newer than the last. This
year's, the newest, had no smoke on it yet.

The boat came ashore at dawn. She stepped out; he loosed the line. Neither mentioned it.

The next day A Su opened the shop as usual. The lantern frames on the bank had been taken
down, and a few old lanterns lay on the stone steps of the landing — red, cracked at the
mouth — and passers-by walked over them.

A Su set the box on the table. In it was the year's work, one lamp flower left, greyer than
the rest. She picked it up, looked at it, and put it down.

The river was quiet again. Until the next Lantern Festival, three hundred and sixty-four days.
