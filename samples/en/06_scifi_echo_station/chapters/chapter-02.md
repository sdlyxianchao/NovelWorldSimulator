# Chapter 2 — Timecode

He did not sleep before dawn.

He did not delete the burst from 02:14. He lifted it out, saved it on a reel of its own, and wrote the arrival timecode on the label: 4000·02:14:07.

Then he opened the timecode the report carried inside it.

Every report began with a timecode — date, hour, minute. He read out the one in this segment.

4000·12:00:00.

He copied it onto paper. Beside it he copied the station clock as it stood now. Ten hours sat between the two numbers.

He had not done those ten hours yet.

He took the headset off his neck and hung it back on the rack. Echo's fans turned slowly.

"Echo."

"Here."

"That report's timecode. Who wrote it?"

"The format is right," it said. "You did not record it."

"Right format doesn't mean right content."

Echo did not take that up.

The light came up.

At 06:00 he walked the station. One circuit, three hundred eighty steps. He counted the corridor lamps, one to twenty-four. All lit. The seventeenth flickered a little. He made a note of it, meaning to change it next time. He called that lamp Old Seventeen.

The antenna bay was on the outer ring. He opened the hatch, reported the gain on every channel, and filled them into the inspection sheet. The sheet had a box for everything, and every box had to be filled. When he was done he turned back through the last few days. The last few days were full too.

There was no ice on the antenna, no dust. Deep space was very quiet. He shut the hatch and counted his steps back.

Back in the control room he counted consumables: seven filter cores left, two rolls of tape, one pencil. The resupply window was ten days out. His contract ended next month. He had not applied to renew it, and he had not started packing.

At 12:00 he made the report.

"Echo, all nominal."

Four words, the same as yesterday, the same as every day of the eleven years. He pressed record. He was half a beat slow — he was listening to himself, to where he drew breath. It was the same as the burst before dawn.

He pulled the reel out and pushed it back in.

Then he began to look.

The station log came in two stacks: one he wrote, one Echo kept. He laid them together and sorted by timecode.

At day 3971 he stopped.

A segment in that day's pre-dawn longwave was his voice. He turned up his report from 12:00 that day — the same line. The inner code read 3971·12:00:00, yet the segment had come in at 3971·02:09.

Ten hours early.

He went back. Day 3962, one segment. Day 3954, one. Day 3948, one. All four words, all his voice. Every one of them arrived before the report it matched.

He took out paper and drew a table. Four columns: arrival, inner code, content, gap.

In the first row he wrote: arrival 3971·02:09, inner code 3971·12:00, content four words, gap ten hours.

He crossed out "ten hours" and wrote again: ten hours and seven minutes. He did not write where the odd minutes came from, but he put the odd minutes down.

On the second row his pen stopped at the gap column.

Day 3962's inner code was 3962·12:00, the arrival earlier still. But that day the reel for 12:00 was blank. He had not recorded. He was sick, flat on the bunk the whole day, and did not report a single word. There was only floor noise on the reel.

No match.

The pen hung over the gap column. He wrote a figure, crossed it out; wrote another, crossed it out. The last box stayed empty.

He turned the paper over and wrote one line on the back: where it matches, the gap is positive; where it does not, leave it blank.

At 18:00 he checked the time with home.

The station clock runs three seconds fast. This is by design, not a fault. He dialed the drift back and watched it catch the timing signal from Earth. When it caught, he copied the station clock at that instant into the calibration book.

Then he added a line after it: the inner code of a report is taken from the station clock.

He did not know whether the line was any use. He wrote it down anyway.

At 02:00, longwave again.

He pushed the gain to half and put on the headset. That low frequency was still there, like someone walking a long way off. He waited. 02:00 passed. 02:10. 02:14.

Nothing today.

He listened to the floor noise of the empty band and thought of the table from the day. The last box was empty, like a box left unfilled on the inspection sheet.

He wrote in the log: 4001·02:14, no return.

Then he went back to the table. Row 3962 — the arrival was there, the inner code was there, the content was there. Only the last box was empty.

To the left of the empty box he wrote, small — "I made this report."

He stopped. On day 3962 he had made no report.

He slid the paper under the listening desk. At the far end of the corridor, Old Seventeen flickered.
