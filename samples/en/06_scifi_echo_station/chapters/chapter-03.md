# Chapter 3 — Echo Calibration

Day 4001. He was not going to wait for the sky to drop anything else. He would run a test himself.

In the morning he wrote a sentence on paper that he had never said. Eight characters:

The lamp in the module burned late.

He did not read it out. Say it and it becomes a sentence already spoken, and cannot be tested. He set the paper face down on the listening desk and watched the timecode. If that sentence would come early, it should come while he had not yet opened his mouth.

He went through the log, first page to last. The sentence was not in it. In eleven years the only thing he had ever reported was those four words. For now, the sentence existed on this sheet of paper and nowhere else.

Then he went to check the equipment.

First the cache. The station was a relay: a signal came in and dropped into the cache, and went out when the outbound window opened. He cleared the cache, dumped it to reel, and played it segment by segment.

The reel held nothing but his own reports, sorted by day, one to a day, timecodes lined up clean. After clearing it he replayed the last ten days of longwave. Not one segment missing, and each matched what had been in the cache.

The cache had nothing extra in it.

Then the calibration.

The name Echo was not for nothing: before the day's work closed, the station ran a self-check that sent a known sentence out the transmit port and took it back in the receive port, and compared the two. Sent and returned the same meant the station was through. He called the routine echo calibration.

He ran it once. Passed.

He ran it again. Passed.

The transmit port's output did not leak into the receive port.

Then the channel. He switched to the spare receive channel and replayed the 3962 segment. The same few words, the same voice. He changed the channel; the thing did not change.

He went out to the antenna bay again. The mount turned one full circle; he reported the gains channel by channel, the same as in the morning. The antenna was fine.

Back in the control room he asked Echo.

"Echo."

"Here."

"These two calibration runs. How far off, against before."

"No difference."

"And before?"

"When it used to fail, a connector had come loose. You could see it."

He did not ask again. He had seen that kind of fault — a blackened terminal, wipe it and it was well.

By the end of the day he had crossed out three lines on the paper.

Equipment fault — crossed out.

Some other sound source aboard — crossed out. Aboard were only he and Echo. He checked Echo too: the source of a report had one entrance, and it was his mouth at noon.

One possibility left. The source was outside the station. The bearing he had measured: directly away from Earth.

He put a question mark after the last line and did not go on.

At 02:00, longwave.

He pushed the gain to half. The low frequency was still there. He ran the sentence — *the lamp in the module burned late* — through his mind, not reading it, listening, to hear whether it would come out of the headset first.

02:10. 02:14.

The signal came in.

Not his eight characters. His voice, saying three syllables, cut off on a breath it could not hold. He did not catch the three syllables. The timecode was new: 4002·19:03:00.

He saved this segment to reel too, labeled it, and added it to the table. The last box stayed empty.

He took off the headset.

The paper on the desk was still face down. He turned it over. The eight characters lay there, not one of them moved.

He did something else.

He drew out a blank sheet and wrote down what Echo had answered that night. First time: *of after you.*

He looked at it for two seconds, copied it a second time: *of after you.*

The third time he wrote: *of after.*

Then he checked. The third line was one character short.

He stared at the three lines. The third line had no *you*.

The first two lines stood the same length side by side; the third was a box shorter, like the last empty box on that table.

He slid the blank sheet under the listening desk too, pressed together with the day's table.

The station clock ticked then. Three seconds fast — by design, not a fault.
