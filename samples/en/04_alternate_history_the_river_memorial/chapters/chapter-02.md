# Chapter 2 — Measuring the Levee

Before daylight, Pei Ji left the county town.

The sheet marked "A New Reckoning" lay folded in three in his sleeve. He did not go to the office, and he took no lamp, feeling his way up the west levee in the dark. Fog hung on the river; underfoot was rammed earth, and each step he took, he took solidly.

There was someone on the levee. An old man crouched outside a straw shed, coiling rope — the veteran who measured the levees for the county, his eyes still good, three hundred days of the year spent on the embankment.

"Registrar," the old man said, standing. "You're early."

"Measure the third stretch of the west levee."

"The pre-flood inspection isn't for half a month."

"Measure it now."

The old man asked nothing more. He went into the shed and dragged out the measuring rod — wood, one end bound in brass, its shaft cut with cun marks worn bright. He brought out a coil of hemp rope too, a stone plumb, and a three-legged wooden frame.

They walked to the third stretch. The old man pressed the rope's end at the foot of the levee, paced off thirty steps, drove a wooden stake; thirty more, another stake. That done, he stepped back ten paces, set the frame steady, stood the rod upright beside a stake, and narrowed his eyes up the slope.

"One zhang three," he said. "The rod was checked last autumn. It's true."

Pei Ji nodded. One zhang three — not a figure off from the column in the memorial.

He crouched, drew the sheet from his sleeve, spread it on the levee, and weighted the four corners with stones. The top line read "A New Reckoning." Beneath it he added four short lines: levee height, one zhang three; minus flood crest; minus wave height; minus freeboard. Then he laid the brush on the paper and waited for the ink to dry.

"Don't look at the levee," he said. "Look at the water."

The old man blinked, then turned to follow his gaze. On the outer slope stood an old post, waist-high, slanting in the ground — the water gauge. Across it were cut several horizontal marks, each lower than the last, the scores packed with mud years old.

"Past floods left those," the old man said. "The highest, fifteen years ago."

"How far is that mark below the crest?"

The old man walked over, laid the rod against the gauge post, and measured down from the highest score to the crest, and stopped.

"One chi and two cun."

Pei Ji said nothing.

He had carried the sum three times in his head, and he carried it once more here on the levee. Flood crest, wave height, freeboard, each subtracted in turn — and what was left was this one chi and two cun. It was not on paper. It was on this post, cut into it, under fifteen years of mud.

"That year the water came this high," the old man said, knocking the score with the back of his hand. "One chi more and it would have gone over."

"And this year?"

"The wind's wrong this year." The old man glanced upstream. "The wind is warm. If the river opens early, the water comes early — and high. Other years about now, I'm still in a lined coat."

Pei Ji looked upstream too, and said nothing.

"Who cut the mark?"

"I did," the old man said. "But I wasn't the one who measured."

"Who measured?"

The old man shook his head.

"When they finished this stretch, fifteen years back, only one man came up to measure it. He nicked a mark on the post — a surname cut in, no given name."

Pei Ji walked over and wiped the post twice with his palm. The mud fell away, showing a shallow slanting score, crooked, a single surname.

He had seen that surname. Where, he could not at once recall.

"This measuring, Registrar — are you going to take it up?" the old man asked.

Pei Ji folded the sheet, then opened it again.

"The memorial is sealed," he said.

The old man did not understand, and did not ask again.

"Somebody'll have to measure this stretch again this year." He laid the rod on his shoulder. "The pre-flood inspection — men from the prefecture. The runners say the day's been moved up."

"Moved up to when?"

"Next month."

"Who's coming?"

"Surnames I can't keep," the old man said. "Only that it's the apprentice of the man who measured this stretch fifteen years back."

Pei Ji stood still.

He put the sheet away, folded back in three, into his sleeve.

Day broke. The wind came down off the upper river, still warm. He stood on the levee and looked at the gauge post — the highest score on it lying one chi and two cun below the crest.

That figure had been measured once, fifteen years ago.

In a month, somebody would measure it again.
