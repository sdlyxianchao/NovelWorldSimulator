# Chapter 3 — The Master Register

The county's archive stood in the back court of the east wing, two low rooms, a wall of wooden racks up to the beam. No lamp was lit inside; only a little light came down from a sky-well. The keeper was an old clerk, his eyes gone, his ears still sharp.

"Registrar Pei." The old clerk unstrung the key from his belt. "Which year?"

"The river memorials. One volume a year, from this year back."

"Fifteen volumes," the old clerk said. "How far back will the Registrar go?"

"Back until there aren't any more."

The old clerk fetched a ladder. Pei Ji climbed it himself and drew the volumes down a year at a time. Each was strung on hemp thread; pull one out and a layer of dust sifted off the racks. The last, farthest in, was yellow through — the edition of fifteen years ago.

He stacked the fifteen on the desk and began with this year's.

Each volume: the first page gave the levee's length, the second the stretches, the third the heights. The figure for the third stretch of the west levee fell in the same place every year.

This year, one zhang three.

Last year, one zhang three.

The year before, one zhang three.

Page by page he went back, to the volume of fifteen years ago, and it was still: one zhang three.

Fifteen one-zhang-threes, not one off.

Under a corner of the desk lay another volume, thinner, not by year but by stretch. The third stretch of the west levee had one line to itself: the year built, the year measured, the measurer's name. This, the old clerk said, was the master register — the inspection men read it first, and the memorial after.

Pei Ji took a strip of narrow paper, ruled fifteen columns across it, and filled one figure to a column — and at the end, all fifteen were identical.

He set down the brush and turned to the old clerk.

"Was this stretch ever raised in between?"

"Never," the old clerk said. "Built once, never touched."

"Then this figure—"

He swallowed the rest of the sentence. The levee had not moved; the figure had not moved. Not measured once a year — copied once a year. Copied for fifteen years, straight down the line.

He turned to the last page of each volume. Three lines of signatures: one for the river registrar, one for the secretary, one for the measurer. Beneath the three lines, the county's seal.

The registrars had changed four times, the secretaries three, the measurers twice. Names for all of them, each his own. The seal was the same one, its corners worn round, reddening year after year.

Only in the volume of fifteen years ago, on the measurer's line, stood a surname and no given name. And turning forward, wherever the measurer's line was signed, there too — the same surname.

Stroke after stroke, as if one hand had written them. A seal can change hands; a hand cannot.

That surname he had seen this morning on the gauge post, cut shallow.

Pei Ji said nothing. He copied the surname down, beneath the fifteen one-zhang-threes, and drew a vertical line beside it, joining the fifteen columns to that one surname.

The old clerk leaned in for a look.

"What does the Registrar want with that?"

"To work a sum."

"The sums are all written in the books. Any volume the Registrar wants, he has only to take it."

"What's written is the sum," Pei Ji said. "The people standing behind the sum have to be counted one by one."

"The people are on the last page too."

"Those written are the officials," Pei Ji said. "The measurer is not an official. He stands off to one side, on a single line."

The old clerk said no more, and leaned the ladder against the wall.

Pei Ji folded the narrow paper and slipped it into his sleeve.

He was rising to go when a runner came into the court, straight for the archive door, mud still on his boots.

"Is Registrar Pei here?" the runner said, out of breath. "The magistrate's orders — the river memorials of every year, to the back hall by tomorrow morning. All fifteen. Every one."

Pei Ji did not move.

"What's the memorial for?"

"The pre-flood inspection," the runner said. "Early this year. The prefecture wants the master register."

Pei Ji glanced at the fifteen volumes on the desk, then at the old clerk.

"The master register is here too."

"The magistrate says send the master register with them."

The old clerk closed his hand around the key and looked at Pei Ji, and said nothing.

Pei Ji pressed the paper in his sleeve and flattened it.

"Understood," he said.
