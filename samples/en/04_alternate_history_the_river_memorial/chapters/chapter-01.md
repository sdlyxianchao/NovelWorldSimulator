# Chapter 1 — One Chi and Two Cun

Pei Ji pushed the abacus away and pulled it back.

It was missing a bead. The fifteenth rod stood empty — he had dropped that bead on the
levee ten years ago and searched half the night without finding it, and the next morning
he came back with the same abacus anyway. The county said it was his temper: dropped is
dropped, no replacing it.

The old memorial lay open on the desk. The paper was fifteen years old, fragile at the
corners; it had to be turned with a palm underneath. He read column by column. At the third
stretch of the west levee he stopped.

"Levee height, one zhang three," he recited.

Then he took up his brush and wrote: one zhang three, minus flood crest, minus wave height,
minus freeboard — and at the fourth line the tip of the brush stopped.

He calculated again. And again.

The third time, he laid the abacus flat and moved the beads one by one. When he reached the
fifteenth rod his finger pressed down out of habit on the empty space and found nothing.

"One chi and two cun short," he said.

Outside, someone struck the watch. He stood and opened the window a crack. The wind was
warm. The autumn flood wind should not be warm. Warm wind meant an early melt upstream —
water would come sooner, and higher.

He sat down again, closed the memorial, and pressed his palm flat against the cover,
as though negotiating with a fifteen-year-old sheet of paper.

"The memorial is sealed," he said quietly, as if he needed to convince himself first.

Then he dipped the brush full and wrote the first line on the blank sheet beside him:
**A New Reckoning.**
