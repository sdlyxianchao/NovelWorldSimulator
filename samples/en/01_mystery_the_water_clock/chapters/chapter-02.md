# Chapter 2 — The Gate Closed Early

Before daylight, Shen Yan went back to the Clock Room.

No lamp was lit in the duty room. The water on the bronze stand was still moving — a drop, a drop, into the next vessel, very quiet. The float lay on the scale and had not moved a hair all night.

He struck the fire-steel with his right hand and lit the lamp. A row of ledgers hung on the wall, one to a year, a volume to a season, strung on hemp thread. He pulled down last year's and spread it on the desk.

The clock-check ledger. A page to a day; ruled across with the date, the hour, the change, the reason, the signature.

The nineteenth of the twelfth month. The first line read "seep at the vessel's base"; the second, "water fast by three quarters, checked." Below it were a name and a seal.

The young artisan came in with hot water and glanced at it.

"Doctor, the Pingkang case — the office has written it as an accident."

"Mm."

"Then why are you reading last year's ledger?"

Shen Yan turned the ledger around and pointed at the two lines.

"In Chongren Ward, a gatekeeper died that day."

"Froze to death," the artisan said. "The inquest wrote cold of the night."

"He was on duty," Shen Yan said. "A man who kept that gate twenty years, dead outside it at night."

The water in the artisan's hands shook.

"The gate was barred before the lamps were lit," Shen Yan said, turning the wick low. "By midnight he was still outside it."

"Could he have stepped away a while?"

"A man on duty does not leave his gate," he said.

He put out the lamp and went out.

When the sun was up, the West Market had opened. Liu Niang's flatbread stall stood outside the tea shed; her voice carried over half a street.

"Doctor Shen." She handed him a cake first. "Hot."

He took it and counted coins from his sleeve.

"Three too many," Liu Niang said.

"I didn't overpay."

"I miscounted. Three too many." She pressed the three coins back into his palm, looking elsewhere. "You want to ask about the nineteenth of the twelfth month."

Shen Yan did not push them back. He knew they would not go.

"Chongren Ward. The gatekeeper."

Liu Niang's hands did not stop — kneading, scattering sesame.

"The bar dropped early that day," she said. "I was taking a message into Chongren. Just past lamp-lighting the bar went down. Any other year, a quarter hour later."

"You remember?"

"I go there forty times a year," she said. "I can hear a quarter hour."

"And after the bar dropped?"

"Someone knocked." Liu Niang said. "Through the wall I heard the old man answer, lift the bar. The gate opened a hand's width, and he put his head out."

"To see what?"

"Wind was high. I didn't stop," she said. "Next day I heard he hadn't come back into the ward."

"Was the one who knocked a man or a woman?"

"Couldn't tell. Just the one knock."

Shen Yan stood, watching the steam off the tea shed scatter in the sun.

"That day," he said, "the water ran three quarters fast."

Liu Niang did not understand, and did not ask. She handed over the second cake and added one thing more.

"The office came to ask twice," she said. "The first time they asked which watch it was. The second time they didn't come past the door."

Shen Yan nodded.

Back in the Clock Room, he spread the ledger on the desk again.

Three quarters. The ward gate closed by the clock; the clock ran fast, so the gate closed early. The gate was the gatekeeper's — his to close. The gate closed, and he was outside it.

He put his head out because someone had knocked. Which side the knocker stood on, Liu Niang had not seen. But the bar was on the inside — and the only one who could drop it again after he stepped out was someone inside the gate.

Three quarters is not long. Long enough for a knock, long enough to shut a man outside, long enough to leave him knocking until dawn.

That night was cold. He knocked half the night. No one opened.

The inquest measured by the night drum. The drum ran three quarters fast, and everyone spoke by the drum: who was where at which watch all went crooked. The case lost its footing, and was set aside.

At the end of the file was the gatekeeper's name, the ink gone faint. No one had turned past it again.

Shen Yan took a sheet of paper and wrote the date in the first box, the ward in the second, then one line of the case.

Before the ink dried, he turned back through the ledger.

The second month. The seventh. The ninth. Page after page, the small characters "checked."

One stroke. Two. Three.

By nightfall there were five lines on the paper.

He went through all of last year's volume, and did not find the night of Pingkang.

That night, the ledger was blank.
