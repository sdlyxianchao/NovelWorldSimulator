# Chapter 3 — The Blank Page

The next day, Shen Yan went to Pingkang Ward.

Two years before, in the fifth month, a medicine seller had died here. The inquest wrote "a fall" — the back court was slick, the man came down on the back of his head. The file was set aside; no one asked again.

The shop had changed hands; it still sold medicine. In the back court stood a jar for catching rain, and the ring of its rim facing inward was brighter than the rest.

Shen Yan asked the shophand: "The master before — did he live alone?"

"With a young apprentice," the shophand said. "When the master died, the apprentice left."

"Left for where?"

"Don't know," the shophand said. "No one asked."

"And after?"

"He came back once," the shophand said. "Stood in the back court, looked at that jar, stood a long while, and left again."

Shen Yan glanced at the jar. The jar was still there. The case was gone.

The file said he had died at the hour of Shen. But the ledger for that day said the water had run four quarters slow. Four quarters make half a watch, once it lands on a man — at which hour he truly died, no one could say.

It was not that no one saw. It was that no one would do the sum.

He turned in to Qinren Ward.

Three years before, in the seventh month, a water-carrier had died inside the ward gate; the inquest wrote "heatstroke." That day the water ran two quarters fast, and the gate opened early. When he carried his empty yoke out, it was still dark.

The sun not yet up, and a man cannot have heatstroke.

Shen Yan stood a while at the gate. The gate was newly painted; the sill was old, and worn into a groove by carrying-poles.

The first page of the file had the wrong hour to begin with, and nothing after it could stand. The case had sat for three years.

Back in the Clock Room, the afternoon was already past. He spread the sheet of paper out. The five lines copied some days before were still pinned at the corner.

He pulled down older ledgers. The year before last, and the year before that; the hemp thread had gone slack and gathered dust.

At the seventh entry he stopped his brush.

Seven lines lay on the paper:

Third year past, second month, Xuanyang Ward. Third year past, seventh month, Qinren Ward. Two years past, fifth month, Pingkang Ward. Two years past, ninth month, Anren Ward. A year past, third month, Changle Ward. A year past, ninth month, Fengyi Ward. A year past, twelfth month, Chongren Ward.

Seven small lines; the names signed were not one man. The hands differed, and the seals differed.

But all seven changes fell on the day before the death, or the day itself. None was large — just enough to shift one day's hours by half a notch.

To mend a leaking pot, the water must be stopped half a day. On these seven pages nothing was moved but half a notch; that is not the work it would take.

In the "reason" column, the same few words came back around — "pot grown old," "water unsteady" — one sentence doing duty for seven.

Half a notch, and it is enough for a man to make it in time, or to miss.

Seven lines, in seven wards. Copied onto one sheet, the sheet would not hold them; he took a larger one.

The earliest line was over three years back, the latest a year back. In the year since, the ledger had stayed clean.

The young artisan came in to trim the lamp and saw him still writing.

"Doctor, the office has pressed again — when will the Pingkang cases close?"

"Close them," Shen Yan said. "Accidents."

"Then what are you writing this for?"

Shen Yan did not answer. He set down the brush and looked at the seven lines.

A film of soot had gathered on the lamp glass. He rose, walked to the far end of the shelf, and drew out the oldest ledger. The cover was worn pale. The one from ten years ago.

He opened it to the middle. The paper had gone yellow.

Then a page was blank.

The page had been cut out; only a fine burr remained at the spine, thin as a thread. Cut cleanly, not torn.

Shen Yan held the blank place up to the lamp. What had been written on that page, he did not know. When it was cut, the ledger did not say.

He raised the ledger higher, then lowered it. In the gutter, the missing page was not folded in either.

He turned further back: the writing was all in order; and forward, too. This was the only page gone.

He closed the ledger and put it back on the shelf.

Outside, the night drum sounded. He counted.

Not fast. Not slow.
