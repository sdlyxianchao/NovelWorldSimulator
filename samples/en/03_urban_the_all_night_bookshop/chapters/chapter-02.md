# Chapter 2 — The Paper on the Door

In the daytime a sheet of paper appeared on the door.

A4, laminated, taped to the outside of the glass, pressed down at all four corners with clear
tape. Zhou Chi woke in the afternoon — he slept by day and opened the shop by night. Standing
inside, he read the paper through.

The lease was up, not to be renewed. The whole street was marked for redevelopment.

The last line was a date. A month away. It was signed by the district office, with a red stamp
under it.

He read it twice. There was not much to take stock of. Everything on the shelves was what
nobody else stocked — dead titles, cold titles, out-of-print things — half of them hauled back
from the publisher in those years, half collected slowly since. He took out his phone and
called the landlord twice. The first time the line was busy; the second time nobody picked up.
He set the phone face down on the counter.

He opened the desk calendar again, to the page a month out, and circled the date with a pen.
There was not much ink left; he had to go over it twice before it showed.

At nearly two the landlord called back. Three sentences: the date was the date; the
compensation was by the square meter; the shops ahead had all gone. Zhou Chi said yes three
times and hung up. He lifted the doormat, shook it out, and turned it over — the other side
was dry too.

In the evening it rained.

He opened as usual, switching the lamps on one by one, leaving the sign lamp alone. One letter
on the sign was dead; from a distance it read “NIGHT B OK.” He did not fix it, and did not
replace it.

He walked the shelves. Three years on, the back row was the one he knew best — the few missing
there were all bought by the person in the gray-green coat who came at three.

A little after one, a man came in, trouser cuffs wet. He walked half a circle around the shelves
and asked, “Which one’s good?”

“That one hasn’t been read yet,” Zhou Chi said, sliding a book onto the counter.

The man leafed through it, put it down, did not buy it, and left. The bell swung twice.

Zhou Chi took the handled-over book back to the shelves and returned it to its old slot. It was
still dim.

That late, his was the only light left on the street. The two shops across had pulled their
shutters down; the street lamps stood every few steps, and one of them was out. The rain laid
two bands of light on the ground.

At seven minutes past three, the bell rang.

She closed her umbrella, shook it twice, leaned it against the shoe rack. Gray-green coat, one
shoulder wet. Rain ran off the tip and dripped onto the mat. One drop. Another.

“There’s one making noise today,” Zhou Chi said.

“Which one?”

“Back row, second shelf. The dimmest one today.”

She went over, pulled the book out, leafed through it, and brought it to the counter. An old
novel, blue cloth cover, corners curled. She put the money on the counter.

One coin over.

Zhou Chi bagged it in brown paper. Brown paper was the only kind of bag the shop had left, off a
roll, one torn off at a time. He did not make change; she did not wait for it. She nudged the
extra coin toward the corner of the counter, as if to keep it from being knocked off.

She did not leave. From her bag she took another brown paper bag and set it on the counter,
pushing it a little toward him.

“Finished it,” she said.

Zhou Chi opened the bag. It was last year’s book, a dark-green hardcover, the corners pressed
flat, no creases. He had taped it himself; the strip was crooked. He recognized it. There was a
small water mark along the page edges, dried, not wrinkled.

When the book came back, he heard it. Brighter than before.

“Where does it go.”

“Put it back.”

He carried it to the back row. The second shelf had a gap — it was the book’s own place. He slid
it in. The row around it was dim; it gave off one thin line of light. He stepped back to the
counter and looked at that shelf a moment longer.

When he got back to the counter, she was gone. One coin and an empty brown paper bag were on it.
He folded the empty bag and pressed it under the desk calendar — together with the paper from
the daytime. The calendar was thin; a corner of the paper still showed.
