# Chapter 3 — The Second Shelf

The breakfast place at the corner of the street had been open yesterday. Today its shutter was
down and a sheet of paper was taped to it, the same as the one on the bookshop.

Zhou Chi went out in the afternoon to buy things, and on the way back he looked along the
street. The hardware shop, the shoe-repair place — each had one of those papers too. The old
shoe-mender had set a little stool and a wooden box outside his door and sat there, not mending
shoes, just sitting. There was a puddle on the ground; yesterday’s rain had not dried. Someone
was measuring the doorframes with a tape, one measure, one note.

At the hardware shop the owner was packing hinges into a cardboard box and did not look up.
“You got one too?”

“Mm.”

“Where are you moving to?”

“Not decided.”

The owner did not ask again, bent to his packing. Zhou Chi walked on. The street was quiet;
there was only the sound of the tape hitting the doorframes.

Back in the shop, he wiped down the back row. Not much dust. The second shelf had one gap. He
folded the cot he slept on by day and leaned it by the counter. He dragged two cardboard boxes
out of the back room, looked at them, and dragged them back. On the lids were dates from several
years ago, in his own handwriting. He left the shelves alone.

It did not rain that night. He washed two cups — usually he used only one; the other had sat at
the very back of the cupboard. At three he boiled a pot of water. At seven minutes past three,
the bell rang.

She came in carrying a thermos, stainless steel, a chip of paint off the rim. She had no
umbrella today; her coat shoulder was dry. She set the thermos on the counter and warmed her
hands on it a while before unscrewing the top. Steam rose, faint, smelling of rice.

“There’s one making noise today,” Zhou Chi said.

“Which one.”

“The usual place.”

She went over and pulled the book out. A dictionary with its cover gone, the spine sewn with
cotton thread. It was the dimmest today. She leafed through it, brought it back to the counter,
and put the money down.

One coin over.

She took another brown paper bag from her bag and pushed it across.

“Finished this one too.”

Zhou Chi opened it. An old book with a brick-red cover, corners frayed, an old crease down the
spine. He recognized it; it had gone out of this shop too. The bag’s mouth was still folded the
way he had folded it. It came back bright as well.

She lifted the thermos, poured half a cup, set it on the counter, and pushed it toward him. The
second cup he had washed sat on the counter, empty. She did not touch it.

Zhou Chi looked at the half cup. After a while he picked it up and drank. Not hot, and no taste.

“What your shop is missing,” she said, “doesn’t seem to be the ones I bought.”

Zhou Chi said nothing. He opened the desk calendar to today’s page, looked, and closed it. He
carried the brick-red book to the back row and slid it back into the second shelf. The wood was
old; it creaked as the book went in. The shelf was full again, as if nothing had ever left it.
Last time’s dark-green book was still beside it, bright. She looked at it once more before she
left.

When he came back, she had already screwed the thermos shut.

“See you Wednesday,” she said.

She went out. The door closed behind her; the bell did not ring. Zhou Chi set the two returned
books together, cover to cover — one dark green, one brick red — both a line brighter than
anything else on the shelf. When he had looked enough, he put them back on the second shelf,
apart. He put the empty cup away at the very back of the cupboard. He wiped the counter down,
and when he came to the half cup he wiped around it.

The half cup of rice porridge stayed on the counter, still steaming, almost until dawn. Zhou Chi
did not pour it out. He poured himself a cup of hot water and sat behind the counter, and did
not drink.
